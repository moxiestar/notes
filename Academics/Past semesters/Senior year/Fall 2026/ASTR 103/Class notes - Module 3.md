## **Lecture Sixteen | Doppler Shift**

**Doppler Shift**

- **Definition**

  - The change in wavelength seen by an observer when a light source is moving either closer or further away
  - The doppler shift can be used to determine an object’s speed, but not its distance
    - If an object is moving side to side across an FOV, a doppler shift will not occur

- **Shifting**

  - An object is **blueshifting** when it moves towards us
  - An object is **redshifting** when it moves away from us

- **Stars**

  - Star color does not change based on movement/distance, since their speed is too slow

  - However, the **lines on the star’s absorption spectrum will shift**

    - If the star blueshifts, the absorption lines will move left

    - If the star redshifts, the absorption lines will move right

- **Equation**

- The Doppler Shift equation describes the speed of a moving object as a ratio of various wavelengths

## **Lectures Seventeen, Eighteen, & Nineteen | Extrasolar planets**

**Exoplanets**

- **Definition:** planets that orbit stars other than the Sun
  - Over 5000 exoplanets have been found
- **Detection methods**
  - ==🔵Radial velocity method==
  - ==🟢Transit method==
  - ==🟡Direct imaging==
  - ==🔴Gravitational microlensing==

**Star and Planet Orbits**

- **A planet and its star both orbit around a common point (the system’s center of mass)**

  - The star and planet are always located on opposite sides of the center of mass
  - They always take the same amount of time to complete one orbit
  - In orbit, their velocities around the center of mass are parallel but directionally opposite

- **Shifting**

  - As the star moves **towards** and **away** from Earth, its light is **blueshifted**, unshifted, and **redshifted**
  - **Note that on spectral graphs…**
    - **Radial velocity:** the part of the star’s spectral graph that points towards or away from us
    - **Transverse velocity:** the part of the star’s spectral graph that does not point towards or away from us

- **==🔵Radial velocity method==**

  - A star’s radial velocity is its speed moving towards or away from Earth
  - A radial velocity curve is a graph of the star’s radial velocity over time
    - Negative velocities represent blueshifted light
    - Positive velocities represent redshifted light
  - The time it takes to complete one full radial velocity curve is equal to the orbital period of both the star and the planet
  - **The height of a radial velocity curve corresponds to the amount of Doppler Shift**
    - **Factors influencing the amount of Doppler Shift**
      - The mass of the star
      - The mass of the planet
      - The distance between the star and the planet

- **==🟢Transit method==**

  - When an exoplanet passes between us and its star, it blocks some of its star’s light

    - Transiting planet: a planet passing in front of a star

  - **Light curves**

    - Graphs that show a star’s brightness over time

    - The light emitted by the star is eclipsed by the planet, indicating its size

    - The time between the start of one transit and the next represents the orbital period of the planet

      - **Note that the orbital period is proportional to the distance between the planet and star**
        - So the smaller the orbital period, the smaller the distance between the planet and star

    - **Light curves for multiple planets**

      - When both planets simultaneously block the star, a deeper dip occurs on the curve

    - **Features**

      - **Width of the curve:** duration of the transit

      - **Depth of the curve:** amount of starlight blocked by the planet

      - Equation is proportional between the planet’s radius and the star’s radius

- **==🟡Direct imaging==**

  - **Process of detecting an exoplanet by photographing it**

    - Provides information about the planet’s temperature

  - **Limitations**

    - Stars will always be brighter than their planets

      - **Solution 1:** block the star’s light (using coronograph or star shade)
      - **Solution 2:** observe the planet at its brightest wavelength (though the star will still be brighter than the planet)

    - **Resolution can be low, depending on…**

      - The diameter of the biggest lens in the telescope

      - The wavelength of light that the telescope collects

        [[Screenshot 2026-10-23 at 1.57.26 PM.png]]

      - **Angular separation:** the separation between the star and planet


- **==🔴Gravitational microlensing==**
  - **Definition**
    - **Phenomenon where a massive “lensing” object located between Earth and a distant light source will warp spacetime, bending light rays towards Earth and making the object look larger**
      - When the object is directly between Earth and the background star, the star’s light curve will peak and the object will look the largest
      - When a single star passes between Earth and a distant star, it can cause gravitational lensing, but the effect is not large enough to produce a larger version of the distant object
  - **Curved space**
    - Light from a distant star is warped because the object travelling between it and Earth curves space
    - The closer the object is to the background star, the more the object will bend the star’s light 
  - **Light curve of the background star**
    - **Peak height:** represents the amount of light bent towards Earth that we wouldn’t normally receive 
      - The larger the object, the taller the peak
    - **Peak width:** represents the amount of time when the spacetime region between Earth and the background star is warped 
  - **For stars with planets**
    - The light curves of background stars with orbiting planets will have an extra peak for the planet’s light displacement 
    - The peak for the star comes first, then the peak for the planet
