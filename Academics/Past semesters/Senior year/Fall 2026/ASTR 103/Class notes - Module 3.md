## **Lecture Sixteen | Doppler Shift**

**Doppler Shift**

- **Definition**

  - The change in wavelength seen by an observer when a light source is moving either closer or further away
  - The doppler shift can be used to determine an object’s speed, but not its distance
    - If an object is moving side to side across an FOV, a doppler shift will not occur

- **Shifting**

  - An object is **blueshifting** when it moves towards us
  - An object is **redshifting** when it moves away from us

- **==🔴Note that red wavelengths are longer and blue wavelengths are shorter==**

- **Stars**

  - Star color does not change based on movement/distance, since their speed is too slow

  - However, the **lines on the star’s absorption spectrum will shift**

    - If the star blueshifts, the absorption lines will move left

    - If the star redshifts, the absorption lines will move right

- **Equation**

  - The Doppler Shift equation describes the speed of a moving object as a ratio of various wavelengths

## **Lectures Seventeen through Twenty-Two | Extrasolar planets**

**Exoplanets**

- **Definition:** planets that orbit stars other than the Sun
  - Over 5000 exoplanets have been found
- **Detection methods**
  - ==🔵Radial velocity method==
  - ==🟢Transit method==
  - ==🟡Direct imaging==
  - ==🔴Gravitational microlensing==

**Star and Planet Orbits**

- **A planet and its star both orbit around a common point (the system’s center of mass)**

  - The star and planet are always located on opposite sides of the center of mass
  - They always take the same amount of time to complete one orbit
  - In orbit, their velocities around the center of mass are parallel but directionally opposite

- **Shifting**

  - As the star moves **towards** and **away** from Earth, its light is **blueshifted**, unshifted, and **redshifted**
  - **Note that on spectral graphs…**
    - **Radial velocity:** the part of the star’s spectral graph that points towards or away from us
    - **Transverse velocity:** the part of the star’s spectral graph that does not point towards or away from us
  - **==🔵Radial velocity method==**
    - **==🔴Depends primarily on the mass of the planet, but involves detecting light from the background star==**
      - **The larger and closer to the star the planet is, the easier it is to detect it with radial velocity**
    - A star’s radial velocity is its speed moving towards or away from Earth
    - A radial velocity curve is a graph of the star’s radial velocity over time
      - Negative velocities represent blueshifted light
      - Positive velocities represent redshifted light
    - **The time it takes to complete one full radial velocity curve is equal to the orbital period of both the star and the planet**
    - **The height of a radial velocity curve corresponds to the amount of Doppler Shift**
      - **Factors influencing the amount of Doppler Shift**
        - The mass of the star
        - The mass of the planet
        - The distance between the star and the planet
      - **Finding the amount of Doppler Shift**

- **==🟢Transit method==**

  - **==🔴Depends primarily on the radius of the planet, but involves detecting light from the background star==**

    - **The larger and closer to the star the planet is, the easier it is to detect it with the transit method**

  - When an exoplanet passes between us and its star, it blocks some of its star’s light

    - Transiting planet: a planet passing in front of a star

  - **Light curves**

    - Graphs that show a star’s brightness over time

    - The light emitted by the star is eclipsed by the planet, indicating its size

    - The time between the start of one transit and the next represents the orbital period of the planet

      - **Note that the orbital period is proportional to the distance between the planet and star**
        - So the smaller the orbital period, the smaller the distance between the planet and star

    - **Light curves for multiple planets**

      - When both planets simultaneously block the star, a deeper dip occurs on the curve

    - **==🟢Features==**

      - **Width of the curve:** duration of the transit

      - **Depth of the curve:** amount of starlight blocked by the planet

      - Equation is proportional between the planet’s radius and the star’s radius

        [[Screenshot 2026-10-21 at 1.32.20 PM.png]]

- **==🟡Direct imaging==**

  - **==🔴Depends primarily on the temperature of the planet==**

    - **The larger and further from the star the planet is, the easier it is to detect it with direct imaging**

  - **Process of detecting an exoplanet by photographing it**

    - Provides information about the planet’s temperature

  - **Limitations**

    - Stars will always be brighter than their planets

      - **Solution 1:** block the star’s light (using coronograph or star shade)
      - **Solution 2:** observe the planet at its brightest wavelength (though the star will still be brighter than the planet)

    - **Resolution can be low, depending on…**

      - The diameter of the biggest lens in the telescope

      - The wavelength of light that the telescope collects

        [[Screenshot 2026-10-23 at 1.57.26 PM.png]]

      - **Angular separation:** the separation between the star and planet

- **==🔴Gravitational microlensing==**

  - ==🔴Involves detecting light from the background star==
  - **Definition**
    - **Phenomenon where a massive “lensing” object located between Earth and a distant light source will warp spacetime, bending light rays towards Earth and making the background star look larger**
      - When the object is directly between Earth and the background star, the star’s light curve will peak and the star will look the largest
      - When a single star passes between Earth and a distant star, it can cause gravitational lensing, but the effect is not large enough to produce a larger version of the distant star
  - **Curved space**
    - Light from a distant star is warped because the object travelling between it and Earth curves space
    - The closer the object is to the background star, the more the object will bend the star’s light
  - **Light curve of the background star**
    - **Peak height:** represents the amount of light bent towards Earth that we wouldn’t normally receive
      - The larger the object, the taller the peak
    - **Peak width:** represents the amount of time when the spacetime region between Earth and the background star is warped
  - **For stars with planets**
    - The light curves of background stars with orbiting planets will have an extra peak for the planet’s light displacement
    - **The placement of the planet on the light curve will be the opposite of the planet’s orientation to the star**
      - If the planet is to the right of the star, it will appear first on the light curve, to the left of the star

## **Lectures Twenty-Three & Twenty-Four | Solar System**

**Planets in our solar system**

- **Terrestrial planets:** small, made of rocks and metals
  - **Examples:** Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars
- **Super-Earths:** planets slightly larger than Earth
- **Mini-Neptunes:** planets slightly smaller than Neptune
- **Gas giants:** big, made predominantly of gas
  - **Examples:** Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune
- **Hot-Jupiters:** Jupiter-sized hot planets orbiting close to a sunlike star
- **Ice giants:** big, made predominantly of gas, mostly elements heavier than H and He
  - **Examples:** Uranus, Neptune
- **Earth-sized planets are more prevalent than Jupiter-sized planets**

**Discovery of planets**

- The first planets discovered were not representative of the actual spread of planets in the solar system

**Tidal Locking**

- **If two objects are close enough, they become tidally locked**

  - If Object 1 is orbiting Object 2, Object 1’s rotation period is an integer multiple of Object 2’s orbital period
  - If Object 1’s rotation period equals Object 2’s orbital period, Object 1 always shows the same face to Object 2

- **Tidal locking occurs when a planet gets stretched**

  - The side closer to its star experiences a stronger gravitational force than the star farther from the star

  - **Tidal locking equation**

    - Here, the period of tidal locking depends on the planet’s distance from the star

      [[Screenshot 2026-10-30 at 1.33.42 PM.png]]

- **Implications of tidal locking**

  - One side of the planet is always dark, and the other side is always bright
    - The “day” side is extremely hot, while the “night” side is extremely cold
    - Twilight zone: area between “day” and “night” side

- **Stellar flares**

  - A star rotates faster at its equator than at its poles, causing its magnetic field lines to become stretched

  - When magnetic fields snap and reconnect, a flash of high-energy radiation is released

  - **Components**

    - Flash of UV light
    - Coronal mass ejection

  - **Pros and cons of stellar flares**

    - **Pros**
      - Abiogenesis may require some UV light, emitted during a stellar flare
    - **Cons**
      - Stellar flares can destroy a planet’s ozone layer
      - Stellar flares can strip away a planet’s entire atmosphere
      - Stellar flares can destroy biological molecules

  - **Flare frequency diagram**

    - The diagonal red line shows how many flares are emitted in a day

    - If the line passes through the ozone depletion zone, the flare is powerful enough to destroy the planet’s ozone layer

    - If the line passes through the abiogenesis zone, the flare is the perfect strength for abiogenesis to occur on the planet

## **Lecture Twenty-Five | Galactic Habitable Zones**

**Milky Way Galaxy**

- **Our solar system is part of a much larger galaxy, the Milky Way, which is comprised of…**
  - 100 billion stars
  - Gas and dust
  - Black holes
  - Mysterious dark matter (yay)
- Note that the Milky Way is 100,000 light years across

**Three key questions for identifying communicative life**

- **Where in the galaxy should we look?**
  - **Galactic Habitable Zone**
    - Sweet spot between the galaxy’s center and periphery
      - In the center, there are too many supernovae for life to develop
      - In the periphery, there are too few raw materials to form a planet
    - Planet development is most likely to occur near one of the spiral arms of the central star
- **Is the star the right age?**
  - Remember that as the mass of a star increases, its lifetime decreases
- **How long will it takes to send a message to that star and its planets?**
  - The fastest any signal can travel is the speed of light
  - **Light-year:** distance unit representing how far light can travel in a year
    - ==🔴It takes light 1 year to travel 1 light-year==

## **Lecture Twenty-Six | Biosignatures**

**Biosignatures**

- **Definition:** evidence of life, past of present, on a planet
- **Life affects…**
  - Atmospheric composition
  - Climate
  - The planet’s overall color
  - Absorption and reflection wavelengths
- **Oxygen catastrophe**
  - The chemical makeup of old rocks shows that Earth’s original atmosphere had much more CO2 (as a result of volcanism) and much less oxygen than today
  - **The amount of oxygen increased and the amount of CO2 decreased once photosynthesis evolved (roughly 2 billion years ago)**
    - Cyanobacteria were the first organisms to develop photosynthesis
    - ==🔴There were no multicellular organisms before the oxygen catastrophe, and probably no organisms on land, due to the lack of an ozone layer==
  - **As a result, many single-celled anaerobes went extinct**
- **Snowball Earth**
  - Geological evidence shows that roughly 750-600 million years ago, Earth was covered by glaciers
  - Early photosynthetic organisms removed so much CO2 from the atmosphere that the planet’s temperature plummeted
  - Phase ended when volcanoes produced enough CO2 to restart the greenhouse effect
  - There was likely a dramatic increase in the diversity of life just as the snowball phase ended (Cambrian explosion)

**Transmission spectroscopy**

- **Most compelling method of finding biosignatures**
- During transit, some of a star’s light passes through a planet’s atmopshere, and some of that starlight will be absorbed by the gases in that planet’s atmosphere
- ==🔴Because of this, we can detect the composition of an orbiting exoplanet’s atmosphere based on the wavelengths of light it blocks==
  - Based on that data, we can look for gases that correspond with the presence of life
- **Reflectence spectrum graphs represent how much light Earth reflects**
  - **Vegetation red edge:** red area of the graph where Earth’s reflected light increases
    - This occurs because plants reflect a lot of red and IR light
    - Note that most visible light is absorbed by plants for photosynthesis
    - **On other planets, this pattern might change**
      - On **hotter** stars, we might see a vegetation **orange** edge
      - On **cooler** stars, we might see a vegetation **IR** edge
- **Life on Mars?**
  - **Labeled Release experiment**
    - **Viking Rover collected four Martian soil samples and tested to see if they would consume food**
      - **Sample 1:** soil placed in container with food for potential organisms to consume
      - **Sample 2:** soil placed in container with food, but heated up hot enough to kill (320 F)
      - **Sample 3:** soil placed in container with food
      - **Sample 4:** soil placed in container with food, but left in the dark long enough to kill
  - **Martian rocks were found with evidence that they originated in water**
  - **Martian bacteria, similar to bacteria on Earth, were found**
    - Magnetite crystals were found within the bacteria, a characteristic of living organisms on Earth

## **Lecture Twenty-Seven | Technosignatures**

**Technosignatures**

- **Definition:** technological evidence of an alien civilization
- Best technosignatures are easy to detect and unavoidable

**Options for sending messages to technological societies**

- **Spacecraft**
  - **Issue:** spacecraft take way too long to travel interstellar distances
- **Neutrinos**
  - **Issue:** hard to detect
- **Gravitational waves**
  - **Issue:** extremely hard to detect
- **Charged particles**
  - **Issue:** easily deflected by magnetic fields in the galaxy
- **Electromagnetic radiation (light)**
  - Easy to create and detect!

**Advantages of radios**

- Radio signals can make it through our atmosphere
- Unlike telescopes that detect visible light, radio telescopes can be used during both the day and night
- While the sun does emit radio, it doesn’t emit as much as humans do
- Radio has the lowest energy of any type of light, making it easier to produce and less likely to be absorbed by the gas and dust permeating the space between stars

**Search for Extraterrestrial Intelligence (SETI)**

- Ongoing effort to find technosignatures in the radio part of the electromagnetic spectrum
- These technosignatures could be intentionally or unintentionally created
  - Humans have unintentionally created technosignatures, primarily in the form of radio signals

**Intentional & unintentional signals from Earth**

- Strength of signals from Earth varies based on its rotation
- **A radio message can be sent at a specific frequency**
  - By modulating that frequency, our message can encode a picture
  - In 1974, astronomers used the Arecibo radio telescope to send a message at a frequency of 2380 MHz, modulating it by 10Hz

**Searching for technosignatures**

- **Best signals to target**
  - Signals that are impossible to create via natural processes
  - Signals that are similar to common astrophysical sources and easier to notice
- **Frequencies to Search**
  - **The Water Hole:** stretch between 1420MHz (hydrogen emission frequency) and 1662MHz (OH emission frequency) that has low background noise
  - **The Wow signal:** mysterious huge radio signal detected in the 1970s
  - **The Kardashev Scale:** ranking for technology levels of hypothetical civilizations
    - A type II civilization on the Kardashev Scale might construct a **Dyson Sphere**, a structure that surrounds and captures all energy from their star
      - A **Dyson Swarm** would only partially surround the star
      - They are possible **Dyson Spheres** in the universe
